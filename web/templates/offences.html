Where a penalty differs for individuals and bodies corporate, we take the longest term and the largest fine. Fines are in penalty units, whose value section 4AA of the Crimes Act 1914 sets. "No size found" covers penalties stated another way, such as in dollars or by a formula.

Fault and the burden of proof

For most offences the prosecution must prove fault, such as intention or recklessness. An Act can say otherwise, and the Criminal Code sets out what each departure means.

Legal burdens on a defendant
{{count .Totals.LegalBurdens}}
  • Strict liability (Criminal Code s 6.1): the prosecution needn't prove fault, but the defendant can argue an honest and reasonable mistake of fact.
  • Absolute liability (s 6.2): the prosecution needn't prove fault, and the mistake of fact defence isn't available.
  • Evidential burden (s 13.3): the defendant must point to evidence that suggests a reasonable possibility that an exception or defence applies.
  • Legal burden (s 13.4): the defendant must prove the matter on the balance of probabilities.

Powers to make further law

Acts let ministers and agencies make legislative instruments, such as regulations and rules, which have the force of law without passing through Parliament. Parliament can disallow most of them.

A power to make instruments is each time an Act says something may be done "by legislative instrument". A power to make regulations is each provision saying the Governor-General "may make regulations". The last figure counts provisions letting regulations or another instrument modify or amend the Act itself, known as Henry VIII clauses.
